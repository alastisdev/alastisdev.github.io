I Deleted Every Ad From My Game — 28,656 Lines Gone

People were installing Shadow Blocks and never playing it. So I removed every ad. Then Google blocked the release because I didn't have ads.

The Problem

The number that bothered me wasn't churn. It was that people installed Shadow Blocks, opened it once, and left.

For a while I told myself that's just mobile. Most installs are dead weight, everyone says so. Then I actually looked at what the game did to someone opening it for the first time.

Banner pinned to the bottom. Interstitial every three levels. A "WATCH AD TO GET A LIFE" button when you ran out. None of it was outrageous — and that's exactly the problem. It was completely normal. Normal is exhausting.

I'd built the thing everyone builds, and people were bouncing off it in under a minute.

The Solution

Delete all of it. Not tune it. Not reduce the frequency. Delete it.

What "all of it" turned out to mean:

  • AdMob — plus five mediation networks I'd added because a tutorial said to: AppLovin, IronSource, Meta, Pangle, Unity Ads
  • Google Play Billing — an entire IAP system for a game with nothing to sell
  • obj_IAP_Manager — a persistent object doing nothing but talking to a store
  • Leftover bridges from two web portals I'd already given up on
1,291 insertions · 28,656 deletions // one commit. my game is a match-3 puzzle.

Twenty-eight thousand lines. Almost none of it had anything to do with matching gems.

What I Found While Digging

Two "WATCH AD" buttons — visible, styled, clickable — sitting in the fail popup and the out-of-lives popup. Both called into an ad SDK that wasn't active on native platforms.

They did nothing. Players tapped them and the game just sat there.

That had shipped. To real people. For months.

The fix was deleting them and moving the buttons underneath up. The popups are better now than they were with ads — Play Shadow Slash to earn coins and Buy a life for 250 coins are both real options that actually do something.

The Part Where Google Said No

I removed every ad, rebuilt, uploaded, and Play Console blocked the release:

"Incomplete advertising ID declaration."

I don't have ads. That's the entire point of the update.

It turns out Firebase Analytics silently adds the AD_ID permission to your manifest. I had never called a single Analytics function. Never opened the dashboard. The extension was sitting in the project doing nothing — but Gradle pulled the library in, the manifests merged, and Google's automated check saw the permission and demanded I explain what I needed an advertising ID for.

The trap: declaring "yes, I use it" would have been a lie and made things worse. Google's own warning says it plainly:

"If you declare that your app uses the advertising ID, we will block releases that don't contain the AD_ID permission."

Declare yes → Google demands the permission → my build doesn't have it → new block. On a false declaration.

So the only honest path was to rip out Analytics and Crashlytics entirely — neither of which I was using, verified by grepping all 27 of their exported functions and finding zero calls in my GML. Then rebuild, and check the manifest by hand:

android.permission.ACCESS_NETWORK_STATE
android.permission.BLUETOOTH
android.permission.BLUETOOTH_CONNECT
android.permission.DUMP
android.permission.INTERNET

AD_ID: not present

Clean. Now "no" was true, and I could say it.

It is genuinely harder to ship an app without ads than with them.

What I Almost Got Wrong

1. Declaring "yes" to get unblocked

I nearly did it. An hour into fighting a red banner, "just tick the box and move on" is very tempting. It would have been false, and it would have created a permanent warning on every future release. Worth noting: the blocker I was fighting had already cleared — Play Console was showing me a stale validation result.

2. Deleting the wrong Firebase extension

Analytics and Crashlytics had to go. FirebaseSetup and YYFirebaseRealTime had to stay — the leaderboard runs on the Realtime Database. The dependencies were cleanly separated, so it worked, but if I'd removed one folder too many the leaderboard would have died silently.

3. Assuming "not called in code" means "not in the build"

This was the real lesson. I'd checked that no GML called Analytics and concluded it was inert. It wasn't. An extension sitting in your project ships its permissions whether you use it or not.

The Result

Shadow Blocks v4.5.1 is live on Google Play. v1.0.2.0 is on the Microsoft Store — the first Alastis Dev game on Windows.

No banners. No pop-ups. No "watch a video to continue". No paywalls. No energy timers you can pay to skip. No in-app purchases at all.

The economy is closed and honest: earn coins by playing, spend them on lives. Nothing to buy. Nothing to skip.

The APK is smaller. The permission list is five entries long. The Data safety card says what it actually does: a nickname and a score, for the leaderboard, nothing else.

Why This Matters

Shadow Blocks was never going to make money. It's free, it has no ads and no IAP, and the only way anyone can give me anything is a Ko-fi link they have to go looking for.

That's the point. It's a calling card, not a revenue stream. And a calling card covered in banners isn't much of a calling card.

But the deeper thing I learned is that the default is not neutral. I didn't add ads because I'd thought it through — I added them because that's what the tutorials do, that's what the extensions are for, that's what everyone ships. Twenty-eight thousand lines arrived in my project through sheer momentum, and I never questioned any of it until the retention numbers made me.

Removing them took a day. Deciding to was the hard part.
